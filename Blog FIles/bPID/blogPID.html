Passenger Information Display (PID)

Page created 22 May, 2026.

Last updated 30 October, 2026.


Background

I recently moved to a house within walkable distance from Ruthven Station on the Mernda line. Rather than check the PTV app on my phone to see when the next city-bound train is, I figured I could program an LED display using a microcontroller to display departure times using the publicly available PTV Timetable API. This could potentially save me a whopping 10 seconds to take out my phone and check the app.

Design

The display should at the very minimum display just one thing: the time until the next train. But this wouldn't communicate all of the info that a commuter (me) could need when deciding to travel the glorious Melbourne rail network. If the next train is only, say, two minutes away, I can't make it to the station in time. So the following train departure should probably also be displayed. The Mernda line also runs two different services, either the normal service or the express service during peak times, so it'd be important to convey that information as well. It would also be beneficial to display any unscheduled delays that the next two trains are experiencing, and any planned work notifications have appeared through PTV.

Unnecessary information that shouldn't need to be displayed includes Mernda-bound train information, since I don't really find myself catching the train in that direction, nor which platform the train will be arriving on because, unless something has gone drastically wrong, it should always arrive at Platform 1. The Mernda line does not change direction in the City Loop like some other lines do, so there is no need to worry about displaying that. There is simply not enough room on the board to display any further information or reason behind a scheduled or unscheduled disruption.

Now the real issue is how to display all of this information on a _ x _ LED display.

Interestingly, Transport Victoria (formerly PTV), allows anyone to see the mockup of any station's platform PID. For example, here is Platform 1 for Ruthven Station. Just to remind you what a PID looks like nowadays. There is far too much information on this kind of display to be crammed on the tiny LED matrix however.

The first decision to make is what typeface to use so that the information on the display is both compact so that it fits on the limited space I have on the board, and still entirely legible. A secondary concern is aesthetics but actual readable is a much higher requirement.
